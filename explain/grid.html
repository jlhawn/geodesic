How WebGCM works
Part 2: The grid

How to cover a sphere with cells that a computer can march across.

1 · Air2 · The grid3 · A dry sphere4 · The primitive equations

Part 1 ended with a row of columns of air standing side by side. The real model needs columns all over the globe, and a rule for which ones are neighbors. That is a grid, and on a sphere a grid is surprisingly hard to get right. This part is about the one WebGCM uses and the handful of ideas that make it work. Every figure can be turned by dragging it.

Squares of latitude and longitude

The obvious grid is the one on every classroom globe: lines of latitude and longitude, with a cell wherever they cross. It has two problems, and the figure shows both. Color the cells by their area and the ones along the equator are many times bigger than the ones beside the poles, because the meridians converge. A cell that is 1100 km wide at the equator is 100 km wide at 85° north and shrinks to a sliver at the pole itself. Slide the cell size and watch the ratio.

The second problem is the time step. A model marches forward in steps, and a step cannot be longer than the time the fastest wave needs to cross the smallest cell, or the arithmetic feeds on its own errors and blows up. That rule is the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition, and Part 3 meets it properly. The slivers at the poles force the whole globe to take steps ten times shorter than its typical cell would need, so most of the work is wasted. Older models dealt with this by smoothing the poles or bolting on special treatments. The modern answer is a different grid.

An equal-area grid

Start with an icosahedron, the twenty-sided die, inflated onto the sphere. Its twenty triangles are identical, so whatever you do to one you can do to all of them. Divide each edge into N parts, fill the triangle with N² smaller triangles, and carry the result onto the sphere with a map projection that keeps areas equal: the one John Snyder published in 1992 for exactly this purpose, which is the basis of the ISEA grids that geographers use for global datasets. The model does not use the triangles themselves but the cell around each of their corners, which is a hexagon, except at the twelve corners of the original icosahedron, where it is a pentagon. There are always exactly twelve pentagons, whatever N is, and 10N² + 2 cells in all.

The cells are drawn on the same color scale as the latitude–longitude grid, and they barely register on it: the largest is about a fifth bigger than the smallest, with the pentagons smallest of all, against a factor of twenty or more before. There is no pole to speak of, and the time step is set by a typical cell rather than a freak one. This kind of mesh is called a geodesic grid. The model runs at N = 128 on a desktop, which is 163,842 cells about 56 km across, and at N = 64 on a phone, with cells twice as wide.

Nudging the cells into shape

The raw grid has one flaw that matters later. The method the model uses for the wind, which the end of this part explains, wants each cell edge to be cut at its midpoint by the line joining the two cell centers on either side of it. On the raw grid that line can miss the midpoint by 16 percent of the edge's length or more, depending on the resolution, and the worst misses are always next to the pentagons. So the model nudges the grid before it starts, in passes. One pass moves every cell's center to the center of mass of its cell and then redraws the cells around the new centers, as the set of points closer to each center than to any other. That changes the centers of mass a little, so the pass is applied again, and again, thirty times in all. This is Lloyd's algorithm, and what it converges toward is a centroidal Voronoi tessellation, a grid in which every center sits exactly at its cell's center of mass. The figure looks straight down on the north pole, one of the twelve pentagons, where the misses are largest. Apply the passes yourself and watch the numbers fall, and slide N to see how they depend on the resolution.

At N = 10 the first pass does most of the work, taking the worst miss from 16 percent to 12. The gains shrink with every pass after that: eight passes bring the worst edge to 9 percent and the typical one to 1.4, thirty bring them to 8.7 and 1.3, and a hundred more would barely move them. Thirty is where the model stops, because in a standard test of steady flow around the sphere the height error keeps falling until about then and only creeps lower afterward. The two numbers behave differently as the grid gets finer. The worst edge always touches a pentagon, and on the raw grid it gets worse with resolution, reaching a 21 percent miss at the model's N = 128, while the relaxation holds it near 9 or 10 percent at every N. The typical edge improves on its own, because the distortion is confined to a few rings of cells around each of the twelve pentagons, and those rings are a shrinking share of the grid: at N = 128 the typical miss is 0.2 percent. Small numbers, but the wind method is exact only when they are zero, and in that same test the error shrinks sevenfold between the raw grid and the relaxed one.

Where the numbers live

A cell has to hold three things: how much air it has, which in the shallow-water picture of Part 3 is the height of the fluid, how warm it is, and how fast it moves. Where exactly on the cell they live turns out to matter as much as anything in this part. The simplest choice puts all of them at the center. Akio Arakawa, who classified these arrangements in the 1970s, called that the A-grid. His C-grid keeps pressure at the center but stores the wind on the edges, and only the part of it that crosses each edge: one number per edge, not a vector.

The difference shows up in the two operations a model does most. To move air, it needs the pressure difference across each edge, which is what pushes the wind that lives there. To change a cell's pressure, it adds up the air flowing in through the edges. On the C-grid both use neighbors that touch. On the A-grid the push on a cell has to be estimated from the pressures of its neighbors, and the cell's own pressure drops out, because its contributions to the six edges cancel. So a pattern that alternates from one cell to the next can be invisible: every cell's neighbors agree with each other, the estimated push is zero, and the model cannot feel the pattern, let alone remove it. The figure starts with such a pattern. Switch grids, then paint patterns of your own with the brush and see which ones each grid can feel.

Nobody has to paint these patterns. Every step a model takes rounds its numbers, and the tiny noise that leaves behind contains a little of every pattern there is, including the ones the grid cannot feel. Those are the dangerous ones. A pattern the grid can feel gets pushed on, spread out and damped away like any other disturbance; a pattern it cannot feel sits unopposed while the model's other arithmetic, the terms that carry air and heat around, keeps feeding it. It grows a little every step, which is to say exponentially, and after enough steps it is no longer tiny. The first version of WebGCM was an A-grid model, and its runs died the same way every time: alternating bands of pressure along the equator that the grid could not feel, growing out of nothing until they outgrew everything else. Rewriting the core on the C-grid is what fixed it. The checkerboard preset is the purest such pattern on a hexagonal grid, with every cell surrounded by six of the opposite sign; try it on both grids. The price of the fix is the next section.

The wind a cell does not store

One number per edge, the wind across it, is enough to move air around. It is not enough for the Coriolis force, which comes in the next section and which pushes sideways, at right angles to the wind. To apply it at an edge you need the wind along the edge, and that number lives nowhere. The scheme the model uses, called TRiSK after the four people who worked it out (Thuburn, Ringler, Skamarock and Klemp, in 2009 and 2010), reconstructs it as a weighted sum of the across-edge winds on the ten edges of the two cells that share the edge. The weights come from the geometry alone, and they are chosen so that the reconstruction conserves energy exactly and treats the spin of the flow consistently. That is the step which needed every cell edge to be cut at its midpoint, and the reason the grid was nudged.

On the relaxed grid the reconstruction is accurate to better than one percent of the wind speed for a smooth flow, whatever its direction, and the typical error shrinks as the grid gets finer; the worst edges are the ones next to pentagons. The model does this for every edge at every step. It might seem simpler to store the along-edge wind too and skip the sum, but there is nothing new to store: the across-edge winds of a cell's neighbors already fix it, and a separately stored copy would drift away from them and take the scheme's conservation of energy with it. The weights are chosen for something else: the real Coriolis force pushes at right angles to the wind, so it turns the wind without ever speeding it up or slowing it down, and hands it no energy. The weights make the grid's version keep that property exactly, so that the reconstruction can never create or destroy energy however it errs at a given edge, and over a long run that matters more than a fraction of a percent at a few edges.

A spinning planet

One ingredient is left before the pieces can go together. The Earth turns, and a model's grid turns with it, so everything that moves across the grid appears to curve. The turntable shows why. The puck slides in a straight line at a steady speed. Only when you ride the turntable does the path bend, always to the same side, and the faster the turntable spins, the more it bends.

That apparent push is the Coriolis force. On Earth the turntable is the ground's rotation about the local vertical: the planet's full spin at the poles, nothing at the equator, where the ground moves but does not turn, and the sine of the latitude in between. Give a parcel of air a shove on the rotating Earth, with nothing else acting on it, and it goes round in a circle, an inertial circle, with a period of half a day divided by the sine of the latitude: 17 hours at 45°. Watch it for a few days and the circle creeps slowly westward, about 7 km a day at 45°, because the Coriolis force is a little stronger on the northern side of each loop than on the southern side. That creep is real, and it has a name, beta drift, after the Rossby parameter β, which measures how fast the Coriolis force changes with latitude. A related effect steers hurricanes slowly westward and toward the pole. Now add a pressure difference. The parcel is pushed toward low pressure, the Coriolis force turns it to the right, and it ends up looping around one line of equal pressure, never straying more than a loop's radius from it, while the loops as a whole drift sideways at a steady speed: along the lines, not across them, with the low pressure on the left in the northern hemisphere. Smooth out the loops and that drift is all that remains. The figure can show this too: switch it to twelve parcels, started together and each shoved at the same speed relative to the drift but in a different direction. Every one of them loops, but their loops cancel in the average, which glides along the line at the drifting speed, wobbling only slightly because the parcels feel slightly different Coriolis forces at their different latitudes. That drift is the geostrophic wind, and it is why the winds on a weather map run along the isobars. It is also what the outflow in Part 1's last figure would have done with a second dimension to turn into: it would have swung sideways instead of running away.

Now switch the figure from an even slope to a circle. Released from rest beside a low, the parcel heads for the center, but the Coriolis force turns it before it gets there, and it ends up circling the low counterclockwise, swinging in and out a little, rather than falling in. That is why the air around a storm spins instead of filling it. In the northern hemisphere a low is circled counterclockwise and a high clockwise, the other way round south of the equator, and in both the air stays close to its circle of equal pressure. Around a low the balanced speed is a little less than the geostrophic wind, because part of the pressure push is spent bending the path into a circle; the version of the balance that accounts for that is called the gradient wind. The twelve parcels work here too: started together beside the low, each moving with the balanced wind plus a small shove in its own direction, they loop on their own while their average circles the low smoothly at the balanced speed. Try the same beside a high and it holds only while the high is weak. A strong high cannot keep a balanced circle close to its center at all. There the Coriolis force has to hold the air against the outward push of the pressure and also bend it round a tight circle, and past a certain steepness of pressure it cannot do both, whatever the speed. That is one reason highs are broad and gentle while lows can be intense.

That is the whole kit: a grid of nearly equal cells, pressure at their centers and wind across their edges, a way to recover the wind along an edge, and a planet that turns. Part 3 puts the pieces on the sphere and lets them run.
